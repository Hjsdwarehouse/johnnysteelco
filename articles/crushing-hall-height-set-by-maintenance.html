How tall does a crushing hall actually need to be?

The short answer The height is set by the largest part you have to lift out of the crusher — not by its footprint, not by throughput. Owners who size the hall from tonnage usually end up with a building that works every day and stops working the first time a shaft has to come out.

By Johnny · 23 October 2026 · 6 min read

A crushing hall enquiry almost always arrives with the same package: a layout drawing, a capacity in tonnes per hour, and a footprint. All three are useful. None of them tells you how tall the building has to be, and height is the number that is hardest to change afterwards.

I have seen this go wrong in a way that is quiet for two years and then very loud. The plant runs, output is fine, and then a main shaft or a concave ring has to come out for replacement, and there is no clear space to lift it through. What follows is not a maintenance job. It is a roof removal.

What actually sets the height of a crushing hall?

Three things, and only three:

  • The lift-out envelope. The largest single component that must be removed from the crusher, and the path it takes to get out — vertically, then sideways to the door.
  • The hook height of whatever lifts it. A mobile crane working outside the building needs clear height for the boom and the rigging, not just for the load. A crane running inside needs a rail level and a structure designed for that load.
  • What hangs above the crusher. Feed conveyors, dust extraction, service platforms, monorails. Each one eats clear height, and each one is usually added after the building was sized.

Notice what is not on that list: tonnes per hour. Throughput decides the size of the crusher. The crusher decides the size of the lift-out. Nowhere in that chain does throughput decide the height of the roof.

Why owners size it from tonnage anyway

Because tonnage is the number in the feasibility study, and the building is usually scoped by someone reading the study rather than the maintenance manual. The crusher supplier knows the lift-out envelope exactly — it is in their service documentation — but that document arrives later than the building quotation.

There is a cheap fix for this, and it costs one email: ask the crusher supplier for the heaviest component that must be lifted out, and the minimum clear height their service team needs above the machine. Then ask how they expect to move it from the machine to the door. Those three answers size the building. Nothing else does.

What else the hall has to carry that nobody mentions

A crushing hall is treated as a shed over a machine. It is not. Three loads show up that a plain warehouse frame was never asked about:

  • Suspended equipment. Feed conveyors, magnets, dust ductwork. These hang from the roof structure and their weight and vibration arrive at the frame, not at the floor.
  • A maintenance crane or monorail. If one is planned — now or in three years — the columns and the rail beam change, and often the foundation with them. This is the single most expensive thing to add later.
  • Access platforms and stairs. People need to reach the top of the machine. Platforms are structure, and they are usually drawn after the building is ordered.

If any of these are uncertain, the honest instruction to a supplier is to design the roof with an allowance rather than to the minimum. An allowance costs a little. A retrofit costs a new building.

Where the dust and the water end up

Crushing is a wet, abrasive, dusty process, and all three attack the same places: the bottom of the columns, the connections, and anything the washdown water can reach.

Two details decide whether the frame lasts: whether the floor drains away from the columns or toward them, and whether the base plates sit above the wet line or in it. Both are drawing decisions. Neither can be fixed cheaply once the concrete is poured.

Dust is the slower problem. It settles on ledges, holds moisture against the steel, and the first symptom is paint blistering at the base of a column. By the time it is visible, the section has usually lost more than the paint.

What to send a supplier before asking for a price

To get quotes you can actually compare, give every supplier the same six items:

  1. Footprint, and the clear height you need — and the reason for that height.
  2. The heaviest component that must be lifted out, and how it leaves the building.
  3. Whether a crane or monorail is planned, now or later, and what it lifts.
  4. What hangs from the roof — conveyors, ductwork, platforms.
  5. Design wind speed for the site in km/h, and the ground condition you expect.
  6. Whether the floor is washed down, and where the water is meant to go.

Leave out any of these and the numbers you receive are not comparable, even when the drawings look identical. The cheapest quote is usually the one that assumed the least.

The mistake I see most

Sizing the hall from the crusher's footprint and treating the crane as a later decision. A maintenance bay designed without a crane and upgraded afterwards is not an upgrade — the columns, the rail beam and often the foundation all change.

It is nearly always cheaper to build the bay for the crane you might buy in three years than to rebuild it when you do. The same is true of the clear height above the machine. Those two numbers — hook height and lift-out envelope — are the ones that cannot be revisited quietly.
